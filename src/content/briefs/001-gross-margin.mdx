<p class="lede">Every diligence process we have worked inside anchors gross margin to a peer median. Pull a comparable set, take the middle, treat the distance from it as a finding. The method survives because it is fast and because the number it produces always looks like an answer.</p>

We wanted to know what that median is actually summarising. So we took the universe instead of a comparable set: every SEC registrant that filed both revenue and gross profit on a CY2024 annual basis, which comes to 2,186 companies once filers with a missing or non-positive denominator are dropped.

The short version is that the peer median is a summary of a mixture, and the mixture is wide enough that distance from the middle carries almost no information on its own.

## The spread swamps the middle

A benchmark is only useful when the distribution around it is tight enough that distance from it means something. This one is not. <Claim id="A">Half the universe reports a gross margin between 23.1% and 59.7%, and about one company in six sits within five points of the 38.8% median.</Claim> Eight companies in ten fall in a band 64 points wide.

## There is a second population

Read the curve above and the shape is the story. The main mass sits low and broad, and there is a distinct shoulder in the high seventies that a single summary statistic cannot see. On the revenue and margin surface at the top of this brief the same feature appears as a second ridge, well away from the main mode.

The main mode falls at 27.8% margin around $1.44bn of revenue, where 200 companies land inside one cell of the grid. The second concentration falls at 70.1% margin around $654m of revenue, holding 131. <Claim id="C">Across the whole universe, 339 companies, 15.5%, report a gross margin above 70%, a second concentration that a single median cannot represent.</Claim> That is very close to the share sitting within five points of the median.

## Size moves the spread, not the middle

The intuition is that margin falls as companies get bigger. Split the universe into six equal-count revenue cohorts and that is not what happens. The medians run 40.8, 35.8, 44.1, 39.8, 36.6 and 29.2 percent from smallest to largest, which is not a decline. Only the largest cohort is clearly separated from the rest.

What does move cleanly is dispersion. <Claim id="B">The gap between the tenth and ninetieth percentile falls from 72.2 points in the smallest cohort to 52.7 in the largest, with no reversal at any step, while the medians do not decline in order.</Claim> The largest filers carry both the lowest median and the tightest spread, which is the one place in this data where a peer median does most of the work you would want it to.

## What this does not show

The obvious reading of a second high-margin population is that it is a business-model population: software, licensing, diagnostics, anything where cost of revenue is small by construction. <Claim id="D">We think that is probably right, and we have not tested it.</Claim> It is carrying no weight in anything below.
