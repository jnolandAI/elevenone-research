# Form inventory

Derived from a visual read of 72 page images, stratified across seven
text-derived visual types and drawn from 33 published research pieces. Every
page was opened as an image and classified by what the image actually shows,
which in a large minority of cases is not what the text extraction inferred.

Counts below are out of the 72 sampled pages unless stated otherwise. Forms that
appeared once or twice are listed as such rather than padded into categories.

---

## Correction to the input: the visual-type tags are unreliable

Of the 12 pages tagged Chart, 4 carried no chart. Of the 12 tagged Table, 10
were charts and only 1 was a table with nothing else on the page. Of the 12
tagged Map, 9 had no map. The one page in the sample carrying true choropleths
was tagged Text-only. Two of the pages tagged Quote carried no quote, and one of
the pages tagged Text-only carried the densest exhibit in the sample.

The tags are a search index, not a classification. Any downstream number that
depends on them (the visual-type mix in particular) should be treated as
directional at best. This is the single most consequential correction the visual
read produced.

---

## The distribution that surprised me

**45 of 72 pages carry no exhibit at all.** Not a chart, not a table, not a map,
not a matrix. Sixty-three percent of a stratified sample of published research
pages is type, image and rule. The exhibit page is the minority form.

That is not the same as saying those pages are empty. They are dividers,
statements, agendas, method pages, provenance pages, quote pages, framework
diagrams, disclaimers, rosters and commentary spreads, and every one of them is
designed. But a builder who assumes "research page equals exhibit plus action
title" is designing for a third of the document.

**Source lines follow the data, absolutely.** Of the 27 exhibit pages, 22 carry
a source line or an attribution lockup. Of the 45 pages with no exhibit, exactly
one carries a source line. The rule is not "cite everything", it is "cite where
there is something to cite, and say nothing where there is not".

**The reading rail is a minority treatment, not the default.** 7 of 27 exhibit
pages carry one, and all 7 came from 3 of the 33 pieces. It is a house habit,
not an industry standard.

**Titles carry numbers less often than expected.** Roughly 6 of the 27 exhibit
pages put a figure in the title. Most exhibit titles name the finding in words
and leave the number to the chart. This agrees with the text census finding that
titles are shorter and less numeric than the received wisdom claims.

## The forms

### 1. Exhibit page, full width (19 pages)

A chart, table, map, matrix or stat grid occupying most of the page, with the
title above it and the source below it, no commentary column.

- The unit line is a separate object from the title. Every one of these pages
  that carries a chart puts a bold chart subtitle and a lighter unit line
  between the title and the plot. The page title makes a claim; the chart
  subtitle names the measure; the unit line names the units. Three different
  jobs, never merged.
- Value labels replace axes. Gridlines, tick marks and y scales are absent on
  most of these pages; values sit on or above the marks. Where an axis survives
  it is a bare line with no ticks.
- The footnote block is long and honest. Notes running to 60 or 70 words are
  routine, and several openly flag an artifact, an exclusion or a limitation of
  the data. The caveat is printed, not hidden.

### 2. Exhibit page with a reading rail (7 pages)

A vertical column, usually a dark or tinted panel occupying 28 to 40 percent of
the width, carrying interpretation beside the exhibit.

- The rail carries interpretation, never labels. Its numbers are derived figures
  that cannot be read off the chart, and its sentences say what the exhibit
  means. Nothing in a rail duplicates an axis label or a legend entry.
- The rail is 2 or 3 blocks, separated by hairlines, never a bulleted list. A
  block is a large figure or phrase plus one or two sentences.
- The rail and the exhibit share the page furniture. The wordmark, folio and
  source line sit on one baseline that crosses the split, so the two grounds
  read as one page.
- One rail in the sample is prescriptive rather than descriptive: its heading is
  a question and its blocks are recommendations. That is the exception, and it
  appeared on a page whose exhibit carried no values at all.

### 3. Section divider (2 pages)

One word on a colour field or over an illustration, nothing else.

- The divider carries no exhibit, no source line and no body copy. Both examples
  carry a single word, sentence case, at roughly four times body size.
- The colour field does not fill the page. Both leave an asymmetric margin of
  the base ground, and that margin is what stops the block reading as an error.

### 4. Statement page (3 pages)

A large statement, sometimes with a decorative image, no data.

- The statement occupies the top half and the bottom half is left empty. Three
  of three do this. The empty space is the form.
- The emphasis lives inside the sentence. A clause changes colour mid-sentence
  rather than being bolded, and the coloured half is readable on its own.
- Statement pages may still carry the study's source line even with no data on
  the page, where the deck cites the study on every page as a habit. That is a
  house choice, not a rule.

### 5. Framing or purpose page (3 pages)

Two or three statement-plus-paragraph pairs establishing what the document is
for, usually against a dark ground with a half-bleed image.

- The headline statements are complete sentences and end in full stops. This is
  the only form in the sample where display type is punctuated as prose.
- Structure is a pair: statement, then a paragraph of about 40 words that
  qualifies it. Two or three pairs per page, never one and never four.
- The same page recurs verbatim across editions of a recurring programme, with
  only the furniture adjusted. Two sampled pages seven months apart carry
  identical copy and differ only in the kicker, the date position and a footer
  line.

### 6. Method or evidence page (7 pages)

The page that says what the research rests on. Three distinct constructions
appeared and they are not interchangeable:

- **Ledger**: rows of a large figure at left and a description at right,
  separated by hairlines. 3 pages. The figures are set at title scale or larger,
  bigger than the page title itself, so the scale of the evidence reads first.
- **Split panel**: a coloured field carrying the study description beside a
  neutral field carrying the sample facts as a stacked list. 2 pages, and they
  are the same template one deck apart.
- **Matrix or process diagram**: evidence streams as columns against attributes
  as rows, or the sizing method drawn as an equation. 2 pages.

Common to all three: no chart, and where a chart-like object appears it is
schematic, with grey bars, no values and placeholder axis labels. Nothing marks
it as illustrative except that the numbers are missing, which is a real
ambiguity worth avoiding.

### 7. Framework or diagram page, no data (5 pages)

Icons, pyramids, paths, checklists and typographic devices standing in for
structure.

- One sentence per element, and the sentences are short, roughly 7 to 15 words.
  Where an element gets a paragraph it stops being this form.
- No data means no source line. Five of five.
- The device may be pure type. One page's entire exhibit is the phrase "2 x 2"
  set at display size with the two questions that would define the axes placed
  diagonally around it. No boxes, no axes, no quadrants.

### 8. Agenda or contents page (4 pages)

- The current section is marked by state change, not by size. A filled block
  where the others are outlined, or a numeral in the accent where the others sit
  at roughly 40 percent contrast. Nothing moves and nothing grows.
- The agenda page is reprinted at every section boundary. One piece in the
  sample spends three full pages on the same agenda with only the highlight
  moved, and that is its only wayfinding: no running header, no margin marker.
- Numerals are structure, not emphasis. They are set larger than the headings
  and in a lighter tint than the body.

### 9. Column commentary page, icon-headed (3 pages)

Three or four columns, each an icon, an all-caps heading and two or three
bullets. No exhibit.

- A vertical spine bar beside each heading replaces rules between the columns.
  The bars are the only structure and the columns are separated by white space.
- Columns are not balanced. Bullet lists run to very different lengths and no
  attempt is made to pad or trim them.
- Two of the three carry no page title at all: the column headings are the only
  headings. That works only once the piece has established the pattern.
- Watch the ramp. Where the icon discs and spine bars step down a grey ramp and
  then jump to the accent for the last column, the page implies a ranking the
  content does not support.

### 10. Numbered findings or insights page (2 pages)

Enumerated claims, either ten terse ones on a single page or four long ones in a
two-by-two grid.

- These are the densest text pages in the sample. One carries ten items; the
  other carries about 250 words of body copy in four paragraphs of roughly 50
  words each, with no exhibit. Published research summary pages are not sparse.
- The numeral is set larger and lighter than the heading it precedes.
- Where items are terse, emphasis is carried by colour: the claim runs in the
  accent and the qualifier that follows it runs in the base colour, roughly half
  of each line, so the page is readable at two depths.

### 11. Block commentary page (2 pages)

Two to four headed prose blocks, no icons, no rules, no exhibit.

- The page title is deliberately duller than the block headings. The title names
  the page type; the block headings carry the claims and are set in the accent.
  The hierarchy is inverted on purpose.
- Separation is gutter only. No rules, no panels. A gutter of roughly a third of
  a column width does all the work.

### 12. Provenance page, prior editions (3 pages)

The page that says the study has a history.

- Prior editions are marked by name and theme, not by finding. Each carries a
  short descriptive line, never a result.
- The current edition is marked by a state change: an inverted circle, a
  contrasting pill fill, or an outline around a whole card.
- No source line, ever. Three of three, and all three carry the co-branding
  lockup instead.
- Two constructions appeared: circles on a horizontal band (folding into a
  serpentine when more than five points are needed), and photographic cards in a
  row.

### 13. Quote page (3 pages)

- The quote is set at roughly 1.5x body, not at display size, except where the
  quote is the entire page. Attribution is two or three lines: name in bold,
  role and organisation in a lighter tint.
- No source line and no chart. Where evidence is needed, a stat block is bolted
  underneath in a second colour field, and its footnote is scoped to that panel
  rather than to the page.
- Where the page carries a photograph, the subject is not the speaker. The image
  is subject matter, not a portrait of the person quoted.
- A third of the panel is routinely left empty below the attribution.

### 14. Legal or disclaimer page (3 pages)

Three pieces, three completely different treatments of near-identical content,
and all three spend a full page on it.

- The divider rule is sized to the text block, not to the page. Where the rule
  starts where the first paragraph starts and ends where the last one ends, the
  page looks designed. Where it does not, it does not.
- The label word reuses the section-divider treatment exactly. Legal matter is
  typed as a section, not as an exception.
- The text block occupies half its column and the remainder is left empty rather
  than the type being enlarged.

### 15. Roster or logo wall (2 pages)

- Faces are normalised, marks are not. Where the page shows people, the
  portraits are desaturated to greyscale so nine source images read as one set.
  Where the page shows company logos, they appear at native colour and native
  proportion, and the visual weight is wildly uneven as a result. Both sampled
  logo walls make this choice.
- Rows are centred as a block with unequal counts, tapering toward the bottom,
  rather than filling a grid.

### 16. Forms seen once

- **Persona cards**: three coloured cards with circular portraits and motto
  quotes. The card hues encode nothing; they are card identity. The mottos are
  set in a face used nowhere else in the piece, which marks them as the persona
  speaking rather than the authors.
- **Icon-and-definition glossary**: six rows of icon, term and two-line
  definition, separated by dotted hairlines lighter than the type so it reads as
  a list rather than a table. The title states the count so the reader knows the
  list is complete.
- **Chapter opener with overprinted prose**: a full-bleed photograph carrying
  the section word and three prose columns directly on the image.
- **Scope page**: a definition panel naming what is excluded, set beside the
  in-scope taxonomy in the same icon vocabulary, so the boundary is visible
  rather than described.
- **Front-matter prose page**: two labelled paragraphs on white, fully
  justified, lower third empty. The plainest page in the sample, and deliberately
  so: front matter is set to be skipped.
- **Commentary-over-exhibit**: regional commentary columns above a waterfall,
  tied to the chart by matching colour bars under each heading. Unusual
  ordering, and it works because the colour ramp states the ranking three times.
- **Methodological restatement**: a full page spent on a definition change,
  drawn as a three-bar bridge with the deducted bar floating off the baseline
  and no connectors. A credibility form: the study restates its own history
  before reporting.
- **Client legal cover sheet**: default office typography, an unaligned title, an
  indented first paragraph and an oversized logo. Added by the recipient, not the
  author. It is in the sample only because it shows how far the designed pages
  sit from ordinary corporate output.

## Cross-cutting rules

These held across forms and are the ones most worth encoding.

**Emphasis is colour, not weight.** On roughly 9 pages the emphasis inside a
title or heading is carried by a colour change mid-sentence, and on another 8
the title is set entirely in an accent rather than in the base colour. Bold
inside body copy is used, but at the level of a phrase per bullet. Where a page
uses plain, bold and bold-accent together inside the same bullet, the emphasis
stops working: one page in the sample does this and it is the least readable
commentary page in the set.

**A hard vertical split into two differently coloured fields appears on 14 of 72
pages.** The split is never at 50/50 by default: the sampled ratios cluster at
roughly 30/70, 35/65 and 70/30, with 50/50 reserved for pages where the two
halves are genuinely equal, such as a quote beside a photograph.

**The title stack has up to four layers and each layer does a different job.**
Kicker or section label, action title, restatement sentence, chart subtitle. One
page in the sample carries all four and none repeats another. Two or three
layers is typical. Where a page has only one, it is a divider or a statement.

**No page-level title at all is a legitimate choice on 9 of 72 pages.** Quote
pages, agenda pages and icon-headed column pages routinely have none, and let
the sub-headings carry the page.

**Dark or saturated grounds appear on 16 of 72 pages**, 10 as the whole page and
6 as a rail. Dark ground correlates with no exhibit: dividers, framing pages,
contents pages, checklists and framework pages. Where a dark ground carries an
exhibit, the exhibit is on the light side and the dark side is the rail.

**Photography appears on 13 of 72 pages and carries data on none of them.** It
is subject matter, personas, portraits or atmosphere. Where photographs are used
as segment marks or grid cells, they are uniform in size and shape, one per cell.

**Published pages ship with defects.** In 72 pages: a production note to the
designer left inside a chart's plot area asking for a label change; body copy
overprinted on a busy photograph with no scrim, illegible in places; a bleeding
decorative circle overlapping and obscuring a confidentiality line; a staircase
exhibit whose step heights are not to scale but read as quantitative. The
caliber bar is high but it is not perfection, and a builder should not treat
these pieces as flawless references.
