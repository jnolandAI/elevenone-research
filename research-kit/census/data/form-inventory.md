# Form inventory

Derived from a visual read of 72 page images, stratified across seven
text-derived visual types and drawn from 33 published research pieces. Every
page was opened as an image and classified by what the image actually shows,
which in a large minority of cases is not what the text extraction inferred.

Counts below are out of the 72 sampled pages unless stated otherwise. Every
one of the 72 pages was assigned to exactly one of the 16 forms in this
document; the bucket counts sum to 72. Two pages fit no existing bucket
cleanly. One resolved by widening "Block commentary page" to cover a running
two-column prose page, not only short headed blocks. The other, and one more
like it, are recorded under "Forms seen once" rather than forced into a
bucket that didn't fit. A third form, "Roster or logo wall," grew from 2
pages to 3 once a second logo wall (attached to a method page) turned up on
a closer pass. Forms that appeared once are listed as such rather than
padded into categories.

---

## Correction to the input: the visual-type tags are unreliable

Of the 12 pages tagged Chart, 5 carried no chart. Of the 12 tagged Table, 7
were charts, including hybrids where a chart carries an appended table
column or a subordinate data row; 1 was a table with nothing else on the
page; and 4 were neither a chart nor a table: a timeline of prior editions, a
stat grid, a method page's evidence ledger, and a page of plain prose. Of
the 12 tagged Map, all 12 had no map. The sample's only true choropleths
were tagged Text-only instead. Of the 10 tagged Quote, 3 carried a real,
attributed quote and 7 did not: two credits and framework pages, an agenda
page reprinted three times, and a page of persona cards whose mottos are
set in quotation marks but are not quotes from a named source.

The tags are a search index, not a classification. Any downstream number
that depends on them (the visual-type mix in particular) should be treated
as directional at best. This is the single most consequential correction the
visual read produced.

## The distribution that surprised me

**45 of 72 pages carry no exhibit at all.** Not a chart, not a table, not a
map, not a matrix. Sixty-three percent of a stratified sample of published
research pages is type, image and rule. The exhibit page is the minority
form.

That is not the same as saying those pages are empty. They are dividers,
statements, agendas, method pages, provenance pages, quote pages, framework
diagrams, disclaimers, rosters and commentary spreads, and every one of them
is designed. But a builder who assumes "research page equals exhibit plus
action title" is designing for a third of the document.

**27 of 72 pages carry a genuine exhibit:** a chart, table, map or matrix
with real values on it. That is a count taken directly off the pages, not
the sum of the two exhibit-form buckets below. 19 pages are a full-width
exhibit and 7 carry a reading rail beside one, which is 26; the 27th page
carries a real bar chart but is classified under a different dominant form
below (an opinion statement stands beside the chart rather than an
interpretation rail, so it reads differently even though it carries data).

**Source lines mostly follow the data.** Reading every `**Foot:**` line: of
the 27 exhibit pages, 19 carry a source or attribution line and 8 explicitly
carry none. Of the 45 pages with no exhibit, 2 carry a source line anyway: a
glossary-style list page, and an opening statement page whose own notes name
the habit directly ("the deck cites the study on every page as a habit").
The rule reads less like "cite everything" and more like "cite where there
is something to cite, with occasional habit-driven exceptions."

**The reading rail is a minority treatment, not the default.** 7 of 27
exhibit pages carry one, and all 7 came from 3 of the 33 pieces. It is a
house habit, not an industry standard.

**Titles carry numbers less often than expected.** Roughly 6 of the 27
exhibit titles put a figure in the title; most name the finding in words and
leave the number to the chart. This is a read of the sample, not an exact
count: judging what counts as "a figure in the title" involves some
interpretation.

## The forms

### 1. Exhibit page, full width (19 pages)

A chart, table, map, matrix or stat grid occupying most of the page, with
the title above it and the source below it, no commentary column beside it.

- The unit line is a separate object from the title. Where one of these
  pages carries a chart, it typically puts a bold chart subtitle and a
  lighter unit line between the title and the plot. The page title makes a
  claim; the chart subtitle names the measure; the unit line names the
  units. Three different jobs, rarely merged.
- Value labels replace axes on most of the chart-bearing pages in this
  bucket. Gridlines, tick marks and y scales are usually absent; values sit
  on or above the marks. Where an axis survives it is a bare line with no
  ticks.
- The footnote block is often long and honest where one exists. Notes
  running to 60 or 70 words are routine on this bucket's denser pages, and
  several openly flag an artifact, an exclusion or a limitation of the
  data. The caveat is printed, not hidden.
- One page in this bucket runs its commentary in six short columns above
  the exhibit rather than beside it in a rail. Same full-width footprint
  and no side column, just an unusual ordering: commentary first, chart
  underneath.

### 2. Exhibit page with a reading rail (7 pages)

A vertical column, usually a dark or tinted panel occupying roughly a
quarter to half the width (28 to 50 percent across the sample), carrying
interpretation beside the exhibit.

- The rail carries interpretation, almost never labels. Its numbers are
  mostly derived figures that cannot be read off the chart, and its
  sentences say what the exhibit means. Nothing in a rail duplicates an
  axis label or a legend entry.
- The rail is usually 2 or 3 blocks separated by hairlines, not a bulleted
  list. One of the 7 pages breaks this: its rail is a tinted panel of
  bulleted commentary rather than blocks. Call this a strong tendency, not
  an absolute rule.
- The rail and the exhibit often share the page furniture. On several
  pages the wordmark, folio and source line sit on one baseline that
  crosses the split, so the two grounds read as one page.
- One rail in the sample is prescriptive rather than descriptive: its
  heading is a question and its blocks are recommendations. That is the
  exception, and it appeared on a page whose exhibit carried no values at
  all, only direction arrows.

### 3. Section divider (2 pages)

One word on a colour field or over an illustration, nothing else.

- The divider carries no exhibit, no source line and no body copy. Both
  examples carry a single word, sentence case, at roughly four times body
  size.
- The colour field does not fill the page. Both leave an asymmetric margin
  of the base ground, and that margin is what stops the block reading as an
  error.

### 4. Statement page (2 pages)

A large statement, no data. Two different constructions appeared in the
sample, not enough of either to call a single rule:

- **Top-occupied**: title and statement sit in the top quarter, a strip of
  decorative photography runs across the middle band, and the bottom third
  is left empty. This page carries the study's source line even though it
  carries no data itself; its own notes call this out directly as a house
  habit, citing the study on every page regardless of content.
- **Side-panel**: two short paragraphs sit vertically centred in a wide
  (roughly 78 percent) white column, with a full-height illustration in the
  remaining side column. No source line, and no title as such: the body
  text does that job. Its own notes call it a soft divider, an argument
  rather than a name, unlike a plain section divider.

### 5. Framing or purpose page (2 pages)

Two statement-plus-paragraph pairs establishing what the document is for,
against a dark ground with a half-bleed rendered image. Both sampled pages
are the same page, reused verbatim across two editions of one recurring
programme seven months apart, differing only in furniture: a kicker present
on one and not the other, the as-of date moved and recoloured, a disclaimer
line present on one and dropped on the other. With only one underlying
template represented twice in the sample, this reads as one house's habit
rather than a form confirmed across multiple pieces.

- The headline statements are complete sentences and end in full stops.
  This is the only form in the sample where display type is punctuated as
  prose.
- Structure is a pair: statement, then a paragraph of about 40 words that
  qualifies it. Both sampled pages carry two such pairs.

### 6. Method or evidence page (5 pages)

The page that says what the research rests on. Three distinct constructions
appeared and they are not interchangeable:

- **Ledger**: rows of a large figure at left and a description at right,
  separated by hairlines. 1 page. The figure is set larger than the
  description text beside it and in the accent colour, so the scale of the
  evidence reads before the detail does.
- **Split panel**: a coloured field carrying the study description beside a
  neutral field carrying the sample facts as a stacked list. 2 pages, and
  they are the same template one deck apart.
- **Matrix or process diagram**: evidence streams as columns against
  attributes as rows, or the sizing method drawn as an equation. 2 pages.

Common to all three: no chart with real values. Where a chart-like object
appears (in the matrix/process pages) it is schematic, with grey bars, no
values and placeholder axis labels. Nothing marks it as illustrative except
that the numbers are missing, which is a real ambiguity worth avoiding.

### 7. Framework or diagram page, no data (4 pages)

Icons, pyramids, paths, checklists and typographic devices standing in for
structure.

- One sentence per element, and the sentences are short, roughly 7 to 15
  words. Where an element gets a paragraph it stops being this form.
- No data means no source line. Four of four.
- The device may be pure type. One page's entire exhibit is the phrase
  "2 x 2" set at display size with the two questions that would define the
  axes placed diagonally around it. No boxes, no axes, no quadrants.

### 8. Agenda or contents page (4 pages)

- The current section is marked by state change, not by size. A filled
  block where the others are outlined, or a numeral in the accent where the
  others sit at roughly 40 percent contrast. Nothing moves and nothing
  grows.
- The agenda page is reprinted at every section boundary. One piece in the
  sample spends three full pages on the same agenda with only the
  highlight moved, and that is its only wayfinding: no running header, no
  margin marker.
- Numerals are structure, not emphasis. They are set larger than the
  headings and in a lighter tint than the body.

### 9. Column commentary page, icon-headed (3 pages)

Three or four columns, each an icon, an all-caps heading and two or three
bullets. No exhibit.

- A vertical spine bar beside each heading replaces rules between the
  columns. The bars are the only structure and the columns are separated by
  white space.
- Columns are not balanced. Bullet lists run to very different lengths and
  no attempt is made to pad or trim them.
- Two of the three carry no page title at all: the column headings are the
  only headings. That works only once the piece has established the
  pattern.
- Watch the ramp. Where the icon discs and spine bars step down a grey
  ramp and then jump to the accent for the last column, the page implies a
  ranking the content does not support.

### 10. Numbered findings or insights page (2 pages)

Enumerated claims, either ten terse ones on a single page or four long ones
in a two-by-two grid.

- These are the densest text pages in the sample. One carries ten items;
  the other carries about 250 words of body copy in four paragraphs of
  roughly 50 words each, with no exhibit. Published research summary pages
  are not sparse.
- The numeral is set larger and lighter than the heading it precedes.
- Where items are terse, emphasis is carried by colour: the claim runs in
  the accent and the qualifier that follows it runs in the base colour,
  roughly half of each line, so the page is readable at two depths.

### 11. Block commentary page (2 pages)

Prose blocks or columns with no icons and no exhibit. Two different
constructions:

- A banner title over two full prose columns, each column using
  accent-coloured lead-in lines as internal sub-headings rather than
  separate headed blocks. This is the wider of the two constructions and
  the one that stretched the bucket's definition.
- A two-by-two grid of four short blocks, each with its own
  accent-coloured heading, no rules, no panels, gutter only. Here the page
  title is deliberately duller than the block headings: the title names the
  kind of page and the block headings carry the claims. The hierarchy is
  inverted on purpose.

### 12. Provenance page, prior editions (3 pages)

The page that says the study has a history.

- Prior editions are marked by name and theme, not by finding. Each
  carries a short descriptive line, never a result.
- The current edition is marked by a state change: an inverted circle, a
  contrasting pill fill, or an outline around a whole card.
- No source line, ever. Three of three, and all three carry the
  co-branding lockup instead.
- Two base constructions appeared: circles on a horizontal band, which
  folds into a serpentine when more points are needed than fit straight
  across, and photographic cards in a row.

### 13. Quote page (3 pages)

- The quote is set at roughly 1.5x body, not at display size, except
  where the quote is the entire page. Attribution is two or three lines:
  name in bold, role and organisation in a lighter tint.
- No source line and no chart. Where evidence is needed, a stat block is
  bolted underneath in a second colour field, and its footnote is scoped to
  that panel rather than to the page, explicitly without turning the page
  into a chart.
- Where the page carries a photograph, the subject is not the speaker.
  The image is subject matter, not a portrait of the person quoted.
- A third of the panel is routinely left empty below the attribution.

### 14. Legal or disclaimer page (2 pages)

Two pieces, two different treatments of near-identical legal boilerplate,
and both spend a full page on it.

- The divider rule is sized to the text block, not to the page. Where the
  rule starts where the first paragraph starts and ends where the last one
  ends, the page looks designed. Where it does not, it does not.
- The label word reuses the section-divider treatment exactly on one of
  the two. Legal matter is typed as a section, not as an exception.
- The text block occupies half its column on one of the two and the
  remainder is left empty rather than the type being enlarged.

A third page in the sample carries similar legal boilerplate: a cover sheet
added by a document's recipient rather than by the study's own designers.
It is listed under "Forms seen once" instead, because its own Form line
marks it explicitly as not part of the research design system, and mixing
it in here would misrepresent what the study's house style actually does.

### 15. Roster or logo wall (3 pages)

Two logo walls and one roster of named contributors appeared in the sample.

- Faces are normalised, marks are not. Where the page shows people, the
  portraits are desaturated to greyscale so images from different sources
  read as one set. Where the page shows company logos, they appear at
  native colour and native proportion, and the visual weight is wildly
  uneven as a result. Both sampled logo walls make this choice, and the
  second one is explicit in its own notes about repeating the first.
- Rows are centred as a block with unequal counts, tapering toward the
  bottom, rather than filling a grid. All three pages do this.
- The second logo wall sits below a short passage of prose that
  establishes the study and carries a bold caption naming what the wall is
  evidence of, rather than standing alone on the page the way the first
  one does.

### 16. Forms seen once (9 pages)

- **Persona cards**: three coloured cards with circular portraits and
  motto quotes. The card hues encode nothing; they are card identity. The
  mottos are set in a face used nowhere else in the piece, which marks them
  as the persona speaking rather than the authors.
- **Icon-and-definition glossary**: six rows of icon, term and two-line
  definition, separated by dotted hairlines lighter than the type so it
  reads as a list rather than a table. The title states the count so the
  reader knows the list is complete.
- **Chapter opener with overprinted prose**: a full-bleed photograph
  carrying the section word and three prose columns directly on the image.
- **Scope page**: a definition panel naming what is excluded, set beside
  the in-scope taxonomy in the same icon vocabulary, so the boundary is
  visible rather than described.
- **Front-matter prose page**: two labelled paragraphs on white, fully
  justified, lower third empty. The plainest page in the sample, and
  deliberately so: front matter is set to be skipped.
- **Methodological restatement**: a full page spent on a definition
  change, drawn as a three-bar bridge with the deducted bar floating off
  the baseline and no connectors. A credibility form: the study restates
  its own history before reporting.
- **Client-side legal cover sheet**: default office typography, an
  unaligned title, an indented first paragraph and an oversized logo. Added
  by the recipient, not the author. It is in the sample only because it
  shows how far the designed pages sit from ordinary corporate output.
- **Opinion column beside a chart**: a large serif judgement and a
  full-bleed photographic object occupy a narrow column beside a full,
  legitimately data-bearing bar chart. Unlike a reading rail, the two
  halves are not tied by wording; the statement is an opinion and the
  chart is evidence, stated separately.
- **Purpose-and-contents page**: a dark ground split into two halves, each
  marked by a two-tone underline rule; the left is a bulleted purpose
  statement, the right is a set of stadium-shaped contents panels bleeding
  off the right edge to imply the list continues. It does the job of a
  framing page and a contents page at once, which is why it did not fit
  either bucket cleanly.

## Cross-cutting rules

These held across forms. Some are direct counts; where a rule is closer to
a judgement call than a tally, it is marked as such rather than dressed up
as a measurement.

**Emphasis is colour, not weight.** On roughly 9 pages the emphasis inside a
title or heading is carried by a colour change mid-sentence, and on another
8 the title is set entirely in an accent rather than in the base colour.
These two figures come from re-reading personal notes rather than a
dedicated re-inspection pass, so treat them as approximate. Bold inside body
copy is used, but at the level of a phrase per bullet. Where a page uses
plain, bold and bold-accent together inside the same bullet, the emphasis
stops working: one page in the sample does this and it is the least
readable commentary page in the set.

**A hard vertical split into two differently coloured fields appears on 14
of 72 pages.** The split is never at 50/50 by default: the sampled ratios
cluster at roughly 30/70, 35/65 and 70/30, with 50/50 reserved for pages
where the two halves are genuinely equal, such as a quote beside a
photograph.

**The title stack has up to four layers and each layer does a different
job.** Kicker or section label, action title, restatement sentence, chart
subtitle. One page in the sample carries all four and none repeats another.
Two or three layers is typical. Where a page has only one, it is a divider
or a statement.

**No page-level title at all is a legitimate choice on 10 of 72 pages.**
Quote pages, agenda pages and icon-headed column pages routinely have none,
and let the sub-headings carry the page.

**Dark or saturated grounds appear on 15 of 72 pages**, 10 as the whole
page and 5 as a rail beside an exhibit. Dark ground correlates with no
exhibit: dividers, framing pages, contents pages, checklists and framework
pages account for most of the whole-page instances. Where a dark ground
carries an exhibit, the exhibit is usually on the light side and the dark
side is the rail.

**Photography appears on 16 of 72 pages and carries data on none of them.**
It is subject matter, personas, portraits or atmosphere. Where photographs
are used as segment marks or grid cells, they are uniform in size and
shape, one per cell.

**Published pages ship with defects.** In 72 pages: a production note to
the designer left inside a chart's plot area asking for a label change;
body copy overprinted on a busy photograph with no scrim, illegible in
places; a bleeding decorative circle overlapping and obscuring a
confidentiality line; a staircase exhibit whose step heights are not to
scale but read as quantitative. The caliber bar is high but it is not
perfection, and a builder should not treat these pieces as flawless
references.
